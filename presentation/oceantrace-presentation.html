Problem Motivation

Why identifying illegal oil dumping requires both satellite radar and ship tracking.

Ships can switch off their AIS location transmitters at night, discharge oily waste, and turn tracking back on miles away. Satellite radar reveals the oil slick, while trajectory modeling estimates which ships crossed that area during the blackout.

System Overview

Two separate deep-learning models with a clear real-world scope.

OceanTrace splits the problem into two independent stages so each model can be trained, audited, and evaluated on its own data.

Stage 01 · Computer Vision

SAR Oil-Slick Segmentation

Takes satellite radar patches (256×256) and labels every pixel as oil or open water so investigators know the exact shape and size of a slick.

Progress: 8,070 pairs cleaned; 3 published models trained (up to 71.8% Oil IoU); leak-free dataset split created.

Stage 02 · Sequence Modeling

AIS Trajectory Reconstruction

Reads a ship's past GPS/AIS positions, speed, and heading to estimate its likely path across a missing transmission window.

Progress: 2.23M Gulf vessel records cleaned and 379K 2010 spill-window records matched to our satellite scenes.

Project Scope & Limitations

Investigative Decision Support

OceanTrace narrows down which ships passed near a detected slick and reports uncertainty around each estimate so human investigators know where to look first.

It does not prove legal guilt or chemically confirm that a dark radar patch is petroleum without field verification.

1. Prevented Filename Collisions

The training and validation folders reused the same filenames (such as palsar_0.png) for different images. Indexing every file by its full folder path prevents mismatched masks.

2. Binarized Gray Mask Borders

1,847 masks had anti-aliased gray pixels along slick edges instead of pure black and white. Thresholding at 128 converts them into clean binary oil vs. water labels.

3. Standardized Radar Inputs

Radar backscatter is single-channel intensity, not color photography. We removed the two redundant RGB copies and scaled pixel intensities into [0, 1] float tensors.

What We Did & Why It Matters

  • Overcame radar noise: Standard keypoint matching failed on speckled radar imagery, so we built a GPU pixel-correlation matcher that verified 3,605 patches with 0/4 false matches on negative controls.
  • Proved train/val leakage: 19 of 20 matched satellite scenes appear in both train and validation — meaning official benchmark scores are inflated by overlapping ocean backgrounds.
  • Caught sensor mislabeling: 719 patches labeled as Sentinel-1 (Persian Gulf) matched ALOS PALSAR (Gulf of Mexico) pixel-for-pixel.
  • Built a clean benchmark: Added exact GPS coordinates + timestamps for all 3,605 patches and created a leak-free split (2,877 train / 728 val, zero shared scenes).

Stage 01 · Segmentation Evaluation

How we score oil-slick segmentation masks.

Plain pixel accuracy is misleading on ocean radar because over 80% of most patches is open water. Instead, we score how accurately the predicted oil region overlaps the true labeled slick.

Strict Overlap Score

Oil IoU TP / (TP + FP + FN)

Overlap divided by the total combined area of true and predicted oil. Penalizes both missed oil and false alarms — our strictest headline metric.

Paper Comparison Score

Oil Dice (F1) 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN)

Weights the true overlap twice relative to total oil pixels. Standard metric reported in oil-spill and medical segmentation papers.

False-Alarm Control

Precision TP / (TP + FP)

Of all pixels the model flagged as oil, how many were genuinely oil? High precision means fewer false alarms on calm water or look-alikes.

Spill Detection Coverage

Recall TP / (TP + FN)

Of all real oil pixels in the image, how many did the model catch? High recall means very little real oil goes undetected.

What These Results Show

DeepLabv3+ achieves the highest overall overlap (71.8% Oil IoU, +9.7 points over CBDNet) with far fewer false alarms (82.5% precision). OFCN captures the most oil pixels overall (89.1% recall) with a lighter network.

Fourth Baseline & Leak-Free Next Step

The two-stage CNN gate + U-Net from Shaban et al., 2021 (Sensors) is implemented in models/ as our 4th baseline. Next, we will retrain these models on our leak-free split to measure true unseen-scene accuracy.

Stage 02 · Vessel Trajectory Modeling

Using an LSTM network to reconstruct missing ship tracks.

Ships periodically broadcast GPS coordinates, speed, and heading via AIS radio signals. When a ship goes dark, an LSTM reads its recent movement sequence to predict the most probable path across the gap.

Conceptual diagram showing how past AIS timestamps feed into an LSTM to forecast missing positions with an expanding uncertainty region.

Why an LSTM Instead of a Straight Line?

A straight-line estimate misses turns and speed changes. An LSTM remembers recent acceleration and heading trends, producing more realistic paths across 15–60 minute blackouts.

How We Will Benchmark It Fairly

We split training and testing by vessel ID so the model is tested on unseen ships, hide synthetic 15/30/60-minute gaps, and compare position error against straight-line and Kalman filter baselines.
